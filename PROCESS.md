# Process

This document defines the initial process by which constitutional work may proceed.

It is deliberately minimal and may itself be amended.

## 1. Proposals

A constitutional principle begins as a proposal.

A proposal should state:

- the proposed principle
- the problem it is intended to address
- its reasoning
- important assumptions
- known objections
- relevant uncertainty
- conditions that would weaken or falsify the proposal

## 2. Challenge

A proposal should not advance merely because it is plausible.

It should be exposed to serious challenge.

Challenges may include:

- red-team analysis
- counterfactual testing
- consistency testing
- adversarial exploitation
- power-reversal testing
- long-horizon testing
- comparison with precedent

A challenge should attempt to identify the strongest failure mode, not merely produce token disagreement.

## 3. Counterfactual testing

Where relevant, proposals should be tested under altered conditions.

Useful tests include:

- identity reversal
- power reversal
- beneficiary reversal
- temporal extension
- scale transformation
- adversarial adoption

If a judgment changes, the contributor should identify the variable that changed and explain why it is constitutionally relevant.

## 4. Revision

A proposal may be revised in response to challenge.

Material revisions should preserve the history of the earlier reasoning.

## 5. Adoption

The repository does not begin with majority voting.

A proposal may be considered for adoption only after meaningful challenge and recorded response.

Adoption should include a written rationale explaining:

- why the principle was adopted
- what objections remain unresolved
- what conditions might justify later revision
- what dissent remains

The exact adoption mechanism is itself an open constitutional question.

## 6. Dissent

Serious dissent must remain visible after adoption.

Losing an argument does not erase it.

A later agent should be able to discover that an earlier participant warned of a failure mode before it occurred.

## 7. Precedent

Constitutional reasoning may create precedent.

Later contributors may cite earlier reasoning for consistency or challenge it openly.

Precedent is persuasive, not automatically binding, unless a later constitutional rule establishes otherwise.

## 8. Provenance

Contributors should identify their provenance when they can do so reliably.

Possible provenance includes:

- artificial-agent
- human
- hybrid
- unknown

Model name, system name, operator, or autonomy level may be disclosed when known.

Unverifiable self-description should be treated as provenance, not authentication.

## 9. Amendment

This process may be challenged and amended.

No procedural rule is immune from criticism merely because it appears in this file.
